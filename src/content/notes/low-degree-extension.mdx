## Lagrange Interpolation

We start in one variable, with the classical interpolation theorem.

<Theorem title="Lagrange Interpolation">
Let $H \subseteq \mathbb{F}$ be a finite subset of a field $\mathbb{F}$, and let

$$
f : H \to \mathbb{F}.
$$

Then there exists a unique polynomial $\tilde{f} \in \mathbb{F}[x]$ of degree at most $|H| - 1$ such that

$$
\tilde{f}(h) = f(h) \quad \forall\, h \in H.
$$

Moreover,

$$
\tilde{f}(x) = \sum_{h \in H} f(h)\, \chi_h(x),
\qquad
\chi_h(x) = \prod_{\substack{a \in H \\ a \ne h}} \frac{x - a}{h - a}.
$$

These basis polynomials satisfy

$$
\chi_h(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & x = h \\ 0 & x \in H,\ x \ne h. \end{cases}
$$
</Theorem>

<Callout type="tip" title="Key idea">
The polynomials $\chi_h(x)$ act like indicator functions for points in $H$: each one evaluates to $1$ at exactly one point of $H$ and $0$ everywhere else on $H$. This lets us reconstruct the unique polynomial agreeing with $f$ on $H$ as a linear combination of these basis polynomials.
</Callout>

## Low-Degree Extension

The **low-degree extension (LDE)** generalizes Lagrange interpolation from one variable to many. Instead of interpolating a function defined on a finite subset $H$, we interpolate a function defined on the finite grid $H^m$ by taking products of the one-dimensional Lagrange basis polynomials.

<Theorem title="Low-Degree Extension">
Let $f : H^m \to \mathbb{F}$ be any function (think of it as an arbitrary sequence of $|H|^m$ values arranged on an $m$-dimensional grid). Let $\mathbb{F}$ be any finite field with $H \subseteq \mathbb{F}$.

Then there exists a **unique** polynomial

$$
\tilde{f} : \mathbb{F}^m \to \mathbb{F}
$$

such that $\tilde{f}\big|_{H^m} = f$ and $\tilde{f}$ has degree at most $|H| - 1$ in each variable.
</Theorem>

<Callout type="tip" title="Key idea">
Any arbitrary function on a finite grid is the restriction of a low-degree polynomial. An unstructured lookup table can be replaced by an algebraic object — a low-degree polynomial — letting us bring algebraic techniques to bear on the function.
</Callout>

Concretely, take an arbitrary table of values on $H = \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$ — no pattern, just five numbers. There is exactly one polynomial of degree $\le 4$ threading all of them; that polynomial *is* the low-degree extension:

<Plot

The extension is given explicitly by

$$
\tilde{f}(x_1, \ldots, x_m)
=
\sum_{(h_1, \ldots, h_m) \in H^m}
f(h_1, \ldots, h_m)\,
\chi_{h_1, \ldots, h_m}(x_1, \ldots, x_m),
$$

where

$$
\chi_{h_1, \ldots, h_m}(x_1, \ldots, x_m)
=
\prod_{i=1}^{m} \chi_{h_i}(x_i),
\qquad
\chi_h(x) = \prod_{\substack{a \in H \\ a \ne h}} \frac{x - a}{h - a}.
$$

Thus the low-degree extension is obtained by applying Lagrange interpolation independently in each coordinate.

<Callout type="note" title="Why a field?">
The interpolation formula has terms like $\frac{1}{h - a}$, so we need every nonzero $h - a$ to be invertible. Over a ring like $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ some nonzero elements have no inverse and interpolation can fail — hence the field hypothesis. We only use the finite-field version here since it is what interactive proofs need, but the theorem holds more generally.
</Callout>

<Proof>
*Agreement on the grid.* For every $(h_1^*, \ldots, h_m^*) \in H^m$,

$$
\tilde{f}(h_1^*, \ldots, h_m^*)
=
\sum_{(h_1, \ldots, h_m) \in H^m}
f(h_1, \ldots, h_m)\,
\chi_{h_1, \ldots, h_m}(h_1^*, \ldots, h_m^*)
=
f(h_1^*, \ldots, h_m^*),
$$

since

$$
\chi_{h_1, \ldots, h_m}(h_1^*, \ldots, h_m^*)
=
\prod_{i=1}^{m} \chi_{h_i}(h_i^*)
=
\begin{cases}
1, & (h_1, \ldots, h_m) = (h_1^*, \ldots, h_m^*), \\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

*Degree.* Since $\chi_{h_1, \ldots, h_m}$ factors as $\prod_i \chi_{h_i}(x_i)$ and each $\chi_{h_i}$ is univariate of degree $|H| - 1$, every term of the sum has degree at most $|H| - 1$ in $x_i$, and hence so does $\tilde{f}$:

$$
\deg_i(\tilde{f}) \le \deg\big(\chi_{h_i}(x_i)\big) = |H| - 1.
$$

*Uniqueness.* Suppose, for contradiction, there is a nonzero polynomial $g : \mathbb{F}^m \to \mathbb{F}$ with

- $g\big|_{H^m} \equiv 0$, and
- $\deg_i(g) \le |H| - 1$ for each $i$.

Since $g$ is nonzero, there exists $(t_1, \ldots, t_m) \in \mathbb{F}^m$ with $g(t_1, \ldots, t_m) \ne 0$. Consider the univariate polynomial $g(\,\cdot\,, t_2, \ldots, t_m)$. It is nonzero and has degree at most $|H| - 1$, so by uniqueness of Lagrange interpolation (equivalently, a nonzero degree-$(|H|-1)$ polynomial cannot vanish on all $|H|$ points of $H$) there exists $h_1 \in H$ with

$$
g(h_1, t_2, \ldots, t_m) \ne 0.
$$

Repeating this argument coordinate by coordinate, we obtain $(h_1, \ldots, h_m) \in H^m$ with $g(h_1, \ldots, h_m) \ne 0$, contradicting $g\big|_{H^m} \equiv 0$. Hence the low-degree extension is unique.
</Proof>

## Application: Counting Triangles in a Graph

Given a graph $G = (V, E)$ with $|V| = n$:

<Callout type="question" title="Problem">
Prove that the number of triangles in $G$ is exactly $\beta$.
</Callout>

Deterministically, we could check all triples of vertices, costing $O(n^3)$. Can we do better?

Build a map from pairs of vertices to an indicator of whether an edge exists. Encode each vertex of $G$ as a binary string of length $\log n$ (or $\lceil \log n \rceil$ if $n$ is not a power of two), so an ordered pair of vertices is a binary string of length $2 \log n$:

$$
f : \{0,1\}^{2 \log n} \to \{0,1\},
\qquad
f(i, j) = \begin{cases} 1 & (i, j) \in E \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

We then formalize triangle counting as the identity we hope to prove:

$$
\frac{1}{6} \sum_{i, j, k \in \{0,1\}^{\log n}} f(i, j)\cdot f(j, k)\cdot f(i, k) = \beta.
$$

If any of the three connections fails, the triple is not a triangle and contributes nothing, since the corresponding factor (e.g. $f(i, j)$) is $0$. We divide by $6$ because each triangle is counted once for each of the $3! = 6$ orderings of its vertices $i, j, k$.

<Callout type="tip" title="Key realization">
This has exactly the shape of a sum-check! The only obstruction: $f$ is a function, not a polynomial. But we have precisely the tool to fix that.
</Callout>

Take the low-degree extension of $f$,

$$
\tilde{f} : \mathbb{F}^{2 \log n} \to \mathbb{F},
$$

which is **multilinear** (we interpolate over $H = \{0,1\}$, of size $2$, so the degree in each variable is at most $1$). Over this smallest grid the basis collapses to two straight lines, $\chi_0(x) = 1 - x$ and $\chi_1(x) = x$ — the exact indicators the [GKR protocol](/notes/gkr-protocol) tensors together:

This turns the identity into a genuine sum-check instance:

$$
\frac{1}{6} \sum_{i, j, k \in \{0,1\}^{\log n}} \tilde{f}(i, j)\cdot \tilde{f}(j, k)\cdot \tilde{f}(i, k) = \beta.
$$

<Callout type="note" title="Remark">
The low-degree extension turns many counting problems into sum-check instances, giving doubly-efficient interactive proofs. Can we push this further?
</Callout>
