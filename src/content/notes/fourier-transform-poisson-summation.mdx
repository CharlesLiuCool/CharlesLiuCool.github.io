## Problem Statement

We can approximate differentiable functions with polynomials using Taylor Series.

$$
f(x) \approx \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{f^{(n)}(x)\,x^n}{n!}
$$

But for non-differentiable functions, this is not possible. You simply can't take the derivative!

## Weierstrass Theorem

Let's talk about all the continuous functions from $0$ to $1$.

$$
C([0,1]) = \{f: [0,1] \to \mathbb{R} \text{ that is continuous}\}
$$

is a vector space.

<Theorem label="Exercise">
$C([0,1])$ is a [vector space](#vector-space) over $\mathbb{R}$.
</Theorem>

Really, we can generalize this to any continuous function over a compact support.

$$
C(x) = \{f: x \to \mathbb{R} \text{ that is continuous}\}
$$

Also, we can generalize it to be complex:

$$
C(x) = \{f: x \to \mathbb{C} \text{ that is continuous}\}
$$

<Callout type="tip" title="Key idea">
Just a vector space alone isn't enough for us to reason about this. We need some sort of clean representation.

Recall in linear algebra, a clean way to represent spaces is using an [orthonormal basis](#orthonormal-basis)!
</Callout>

In linear algebra, orthogonality just means perpendicular, and normality means length $1$! Here is the generalization of that concept.

For orthonormality, we need an [inner product](#inner-product).

We define the *inner product* for reals $\mathbb{R}$:

$$
\langle f,g\rangle = \int_{0}^1 f(x)g(x)\,dx
$$

Or for the complex generalization:

$$
\langle f,g\rangle = \int_{0}^1 f(x)\overline{g(x)}\,dx
$$

We see this indeed is an inner product.

<Theorem label="Exercise">
$\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ defined earlier is an inner product in $C([0,1])$.
</Theorem>

A non-trivial result is we can create an orthonormal basis out of trigonometric functions:

<Theorem label="Exercise">
$\{\sin(2\pi n x), \cos(2\pi n x)\}_{n = -\infty}^\infty$ is an *orthonormal set* in $C([0,1])$.
</Theorem>

What's cool is we can use linear combinations of these elements to build the continuous functions we need. More rigorously:

Let $V = \operatorname{span}\{\sin(2\pi n x), \cos(2\pi n x)\}_{n = -\infty}^{\infty}$.

<Theorem title="Weierstrass Theorem">
$V$ is *dense* in $C([0,1])$. This means given $f \in C([0,1])$, for any $\epsilon > 0$,

$$
\exists\;m \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ and } g(x) = \sum_{n = -m}^{m}\big(a_n \sin(2\pi n x) + b_n \cos(2 \pi n x)\big) \in V
$$

such that $|f(x) - g(x)| < \epsilon$ $\;\forall\;x \in [0,1]$.

In fact, this also means $f$ is approximable by a polynomial, since $\sin$ and $\cos$ can both be approximated by polynomials through Taylor series.
</Theorem>

What this means is for any continuous function between $0$ and $1$, there exists some function built from the *finite* linear combinations of trigonometric $\sin$ and $\cos$ terms which approximates arbitrarily close to $f$.

As $\epsilon$ (room for tolerance between $f$ and the approximation $g$) decreases, $m$ (the number of summands) increases.

So if we extend $m$ to infinity, we can get arbitrarily close to any continuous function between $0$ and $1$ we want!

## Fourier Series

Fourier series is just the $g(x)$ in Weierstrass extended to infinity. It lets you approximate functions with linear combinations of trigonometric functions.

For $f \in C([0,1])$ in the real case,

$$
f(x) = \sum_{n = -\infty}^\infty \big(a_n \sin(2\pi n x) + b_n \cos(2 \pi n x)\big), \qquad a_n, b_n \in \mathbb{R}
$$

For the complex generalization, we can write even more concisely

$$
f(x) = \sum_{n = -\infty}^\infty c_n e^{2\pi i n x}, \qquad c_n \in \mathbb{C}
$$

The reason you can do this is as follows.

We know by Euler's identity

$$
e^{2\pi i n x} = \cos(2\pi n x) + i \sin(2 \pi n x)
$$

You can show by expanding each Euler identity that

- $\cos(2\pi n x) = \dfrac{e^{2\pi i n x} + e^{-2 \pi i nx}}{2}$
- $\sin(2\pi n x) = \dfrac{e^{2\pi i n x} - e^{-2 \pi i nx}}{2i}$

Substitute those in:

$$
e^{2\pi i n x} = \frac{e^{2\pi i n x} + e^{-2 \pi i nx}}{2} + \frac{e^{2\pi i n x} - e^{-2 \pi i nx}}{2i}
$$

We can interpret this as one coefficient attached to $e^{2\pi i n x}$ and another attached to $e^{-2\pi i n x}$. The latter coefficient is absorbed into the $-n$'th index of the summation, so we see indeed the real case does fit into the complex form.

<Theorem label="Exercise">
$\{e^{2\pi in x}\}_{n = -\infty}^{\infty}$ is an orthonormal set.
</Theorem>

## Fourier Transform and Poisson Summation Formula

The idea of the Fourier transform is it helps construct the coefficients $c_n$ of the trigonometric functions which form the "basis".

Take continuous $h(x)$ with *compact* support $\{x \in \mathbb{R} \,|\, h(x) \neq 0\} \subset [a,b]$.

(or more generally, any $h(x)$ in a [Schwartz space](#schwartz-space))

Define

$$
f(x) = \sum_{m = -\infty}^\infty h(x+m) \in C([0,1])
$$

What we find is the coefficients of the trigonometric function are the Fourier transform.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= \sum_{n = -\infty}^\infty c_n e^{2\pi i n x} \\
&= \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} \hat{h}(n)\, e^{2\pi in x}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\hat{h}(n) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(x)\, e^{-2\pi inx}\,dx$, or the Fourier transform of $h(x)$.

To solve for the coefficient $c_n$, we recognize that $e^{2\pi inx}$ lives in the orthonormal basis. This means if we take the inner product with it on both sides:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle f(x), e^{2\pi inx} \rangle &= \left\langle\sum_{n = -\infty}^\infty c_n  e^{2\pi i n x}, e^{2\pi i nx}\right\rangle \\
&= c_n\langle e^{2\pi inx} , e^{2\pi inx}\rangle \\
&= c_n
\end{aligned}
$$

These transformations happen because for every $n$ (let's call it $y$) that is different from the $n$ in $e^{2\pi in x}$, $\langle e^{2\pi inx}, e^{2\pi i yx} \rangle = 0$ since they are different elements in an orthonormal set (key piece, orthogonal). On the other hand, $\langle e^{2\pi inx}, e^{2\pi i nx} \rangle = 1$ (key piece, normal).

So

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_n &= \langle f, e^{2\pi i nx} \rangle \\
&= \int_0^1 f(x) \overline{e^{2\pi i nx}}\,dx \\
&= \int_0^1 \sum_{m = -\infty}^\infty h(x + m)\, e^{-2\pi in x}\, dx
\end{aligned}
$$

Recall $f(x)$ is defined as $\sum_{m = -\infty}^\infty h(x + m)$:

$$
= \sum_{m = -\infty}^\infty \int_0^1 h(x + m)\, e^{-2\pi i nx}\, dx
$$

Let's perform a variable substitution:

- $t = x+ m \implies x = t-m$
- $dx = dt$

$$
= \sum_{m=-\infty}^\infty \int_m^{m+1} h(t)\, e^{-2\pi in (t-m)}\,dt
$$

Notice, this is just

$$
= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(t)\, e^{-2\pi in (t-m)}\,dt
$$

which is the Fourier transform of $h$!

### Poisson Summation Formula

Notice, our result is:

$$
\sum_{n= - \infty}^{\infty} h(x+n) = \sum_{n = -\infty}^\infty \hat{h}(n)\, e^{2\pi inx}
$$

Take $x=0$:

$$
\sum_{n= - \infty}^{\infty} h(n) = \sum_{n = -\infty}^\infty \hat{h}(n)
$$

So summing the Fourier transform tells us what summing the original function will get, obtaining what we call the *average* (kind of like but not like your traditional average) here.

## Application: Proving Equidistribution of Points

A classic problem is proving that points are equally distributed on some interval.

Suppose you have a sequence of points

$$
\{a_n\}_{n=1}^\infty
$$

and you want to show they are equally distributed on $[0,1]$.

Intuitively, *equally distributed* means the points spread evenly across the interval — every subinterval of the same width gets its fair share. Compare the two cases below:

$$
\#\{a_n \in [a,b]\,|\, n \le x\}
$$

Meaning we count $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_x$ and find how many values lie in $[a,b]$.

For example, take the arbitrary subinterval $[a,b] = [0.3, 0.6]$. Of the enumerated points below, exactly three — $a_4, a_5, a_6$ — fall inside it, so $\#\{a_n \in [a,b]\} = 3$ here.

Then we divide this by $x$, to find the density of points indexed $1$ through $x$ which actually lay in that interval.

$$
\frac{\#\{a_n \in [a,b]\,|\, n \le x\}}{x}
$$

This ratio should be equivalent to

$$
\frac{|b-a|}{1},
$$

the ratio of size of the small interval $[a,b]$ to the larger space $[0,1]$, as $x$ approaches infinity, if points are truly equidistant.

Now if we apply some function

$$
f: [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}
$$

We see

$$
\lim_{x \to \infty}\sum_{n \le x}f(a_n) \approx \int f(x)\,dx
$$

If this holds, then the function is equally distributed. Each point, if uniformly distributed across the interval, properly builds Riemann rectangles and slices which form the integral. If it was not equally distributed, then a slice might be double counted or missed.

To see why, give every sample $a_n$ the same width $\tfrac{1}{N}$ — its fair share of $[0,1]$ — and height $f(a_n)$. When the points are uniform, those rectangles tile the interval exactly once, and their tops trace out $f$, so the slices reassemble the area under the curve:

<Plot

/>

When the points clump, each rectangle still claims width $\tfrac{1}{N}$, but the crowded region stacks overlapping slices (that area is **counted several times**, shown by the darker overlaps) while the sparse region is left with **gaps** (that area is missed entirely). The rectangles no longer match the region under $f$, so the sum drifts away from the integral:

<Plot
  domain={[0, 1]}
  range={[0, 1]}
  yTicks={[]}

  points={clumpPts}

/>

The problem is, the left-hand side is often hard to compute. But we know by the Poisson summation formula

$$
\sum_{n = -\infty}^\infty f(a_n) = \sum_{n = -\infty}^\infty \hat{f}(a_n)
$$

where $\hat{f}$ is the Fourier transform, and that is often easier to solve.

## Appendix

In case you don't know:

### Vector Space

This requires some basic abstract algebra knowledge. If you don't know what a field is, you can look it up or refer to one of my (possible) future notes if I make an abstract algebra writeup. Otherwise, for now think of it as a set equipped with two operations, addition and multiplication, with some cool inherent properties.

<Definition title="Vector Space">
Let $F$ be a field. A vector space over $F$ is a set $V$ together with two operations:

1. Vector addition: $V \times V \to V$, written $u + v$
2. Scalar multiplication: $F \times V \to V$, written $av$

such that for all $u,v,w \in V$ and all $a,b \in F$, the following axioms hold:

*Additive Axioms:*

1. Associativity
2. Commutativity
3. Zero vector
4. Additive inverse

*Scalar Multiplication Axioms:*

1. Distributivity over vectors: $a(u+v) = au + av$
2. Distributivity over scalars: $(a+b)v = av + bv$
3. Compatibility with field multiplication: $a(bv) = (ab)v$
4. Identity element: $1v = v$ (where $1$ is the multiplicative identity in $F$)
</Definition>

### Orthonormal Basis

<Definition title="Orthonormal Basis">
Let $\langle \cdot , \cdot \rangle$ be the inner product defined on the vector space $V$. An *orthonormal basis* is a set of elements in the vector space (e.g. $\{f,g,h\}$, $f,g,h \in V$) that satisfies:

- *Orthogonality*: $\langle f,g \rangle = 0$
- *Normality*: $\langle f,f \rangle = 1$
</Definition>

### Inner Product

<Definition title="Inner Product">
An *inner product* on a vector space $V$ is a function

$$
\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle: V \times V \to \mathbb{R}\ (\text{or }\mathbb{C})
$$

that takes two vectors and returns a scalar. It must satisfy the following properties for all $u,v,w \in V$ and all scalars $a$:

- *Linearity*: $\langle au + v, w \rangle = a\langle u,w \rangle + \langle v, w\rangle$
- *Symmetry*: $\langle u,v \rangle = \langle v,u \rangle$
- *Positive Definiteness*: $\langle v,v \rangle \ge 0$, with equality if and only if $v = 0$

The *norm* or length of a vector is then defined by

$$
\|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v,v \rangle}
$$
</Definition>

### Schwartz Space

Roughly speaking, it's the space of all functions $f$ where $f(x) \to 0$ rapidly as $x \to \pm \infty$.

The canonical example is the Gaussian $f(x) = e^{-x^2}$: it and all of its derivatives decay faster than any polynomial grows, so it dies off rapidly in both directions.
